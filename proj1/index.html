Images of the Russian Empire

Project 1 · Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii photo collection · CS180, Fall 2026 · Tan Ee Khai

Prokudin-Gorskii photographed each scene three times through blue, green and red filters onto one glass plate. Each Library of Congress scan stacks those exposures top to bottom (B, G, R). The scan is cut into three channels, green and red are shifted until they sit on top of blue, and the result is stacked into an RGB image. Every offset below is (dx, dy) in pixels: the channel was moved dx right and dy down. Everything is written from scratch in NumPy except image I/O and np.roll.

Method

A candidate shift is applied with np.roll and scored against blue on the inner 80 % of the frame only, since the plate borders are scratched, black or white, and do not move with the scene. Two metrics were implemented: L2, the sum of squared pixel differences (lower is better), and NCC, a dot product between the two channels after subtracting each one's mean and dividing by its norm (higher is better, maximum 1). NCC is used throughout because it ignores the brightness difference between filters, which L2 counts as error at every pixel. Single-scale search tries every shift in a [−15, 15] window, 961 candidates, and keeps the best score.

Full-size TIFs are about ten times larger, so their offsets are too, and a window wide enough to cover them would mean over a hundred thousand candidates on a 12-megapixel image. The pyramid avoids this by working coarse-to-fine: each level is produced by blurring the previous one with the binomial kernel [1 4 6 4 1]/16 and then keeping every other pixel, where the blur removes the high frequencies first so that the subsampling does not alias, and this recursion continues until the short side is about 200 px, which for a 3200 px channel means five levels and leaves an image on which a +-20 search is cheap. Walking back up, the estimate from the level below is doubled and refined within a +-2 window at each stage, so that only 25 candidates are ever evaluated on the full-size image, and with the window sizes chosen as described each TIF aligns in 11–14 s including the time to load the 70 MB file.

def align_pyramid(moving, ref, min_size=300, coarse_radius=20, fine_radius=2):
    if min(moving.shape) <= min_size:
        return align_single_scale(moving, ref, (0, 0), coarse_radius)
    dx, dy = align_pyramid(downsample(moving), downsample(ref))
    return align_single_scale(moving, ref, (2*dx, 2*dy), fine_radius)

My original intention was to use a 100 px base level for the coarse search, but an image that small does not seem to have enough structure for the coarse estimate to be reliable, and once that estimate is wrong the +-2 refinement at the finer levels has no way to recover, so the base was raised to about 200 px and the coarse window widened to +-20, which fixed every image except Emir.

Single-scale

On the three low-resolution JPGs the exhaustive single-scale search is fast enough to run directly, and since NCC and L2 return exactly the same displacement for every channel of every image, only the NCC results are shown here.

Pyramid

Running the pyramid with NCC on raw pixel values over all 14 provided images aligns 13 of them with no visible fringing, the one exception being Emir, whose green channel lands correctly but whose red channel ends up far from where it should be.

The failure can be traced to the robe, which is such a saturated blue that the blue filter passes almost all of its light and renders it nearly white in the blue exposure, while the red filter blocks that same light and renders it nearly black in the red exposure. The robe covers a large part of the frame, and over that whole region the two channels are anti-correlated: wherever blue is bright, red is dark. NCC can cancel a constant brightness offset and a contrast difference, but it cannot flip the sign of a region, so at the true alignment the robe contributes a large negative term and the true alignment scores badly. The search settles on (−205, 141) instead, where wrapped-around border content happens to correlate better than the picture. Green, whose robe brightness is in between, aligns correctly at (24, 49).

Additional plates

To check that the parameters had not simply been tuned to the provided set, three further full-resolution scans were downloaded from the Library of Congress collection and run through the identical pipeline with the identical settings, and all three aligned on raw pixels without any adjustment.

Bells & whistles

Gradient features

Edges sit in the same place in every channel regardless of how bright that channel is, so matching edge strength sidesteps the anti-correlation that broke Emir. Partial derivatives are taken with the central-difference kernel [−1 0 1]/2 from lecture, and the gradient magnitude sqrt(gx² + gy²), large on edges and near zero on flat regions, is fed into the same NCC pyramid unchanged. With this change Emir's red channel snaps to (40, 107), and on eleven of the fourteen images the gradient offsets match the raw-pixel ones to within a pixel, but on religous_painting and three_generations they land 2–3 px away and leave a faint fringe, most likely because gradient magnitude discards the sign of each edge and the fine repetitive texture in those two images flattens the correlation peak enough for the search to slide, which is why raw pixels remain the default and gradients are kept as the fallback for cases like Emir.

Caveat. Gradient features do not resolve every image. They fix Emir because Emir's problem was the metric being misled by brightness, but on both religous_painting and three_generations the plain translation matched on raw brightness actually ends up with a smaller fringe than the gradient-based edge matching does, even though the two answers differ by only two or three pixels, and on three_generations neither result is entirely free of fringing. This could be due to a slight rotation of the plate between exposures, the subject moving in the seconds between shots, or other causes I have not identified, but whatever the reason it is not something a different choice of matching feature can correct, since both features are still searching only over translations; a richer transformation, such as also searching over small rotations and scales, would be needed.

Auto-crop, contrast, white balance

The border is detected rather than fixed: rows and columns wrapped by np.roll are dropped, then lines that are mostly near-black, near-white, or where the channels disagree are trimmed from each side (at most 12 %). Intensities are stretched so the 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles map to 0 and 1. Gray-world white balance, following the Wikipedia article on colour balance and section 2.3.2 of Szeliski, scales each channel so its mean equals the mean of all three.
