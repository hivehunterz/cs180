Project 1 — Images of the Russian Empire

Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii photo collection · CS180, Fall 2026 · Tan Ee Khai

The setup

Around 1907, Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii got permission from the Tsar to travel across Russia taking colour photographs, decades before colour film existed. Each scene was exposed three times onto one glass plate, once through a blue filter, once through green, once through red. The plates were never printed in colour in his lifetime, but they survived and were scanned by the Library of Congress, which is where this project starts.

Each scan is one tall greyscale image with the three exposures stacked on top of each other: blue on top, then green, then red. The scan is cut into its three channels, green and red are slid until they sit exactly on top of blue, and the three are stacked into an RGB picture. The catch is that the exposures were taken a few seconds apart and the plate moved a little in between, so the channels don't line up out of the box. A shift that is wrong by even a couple of pixels leaves colour fringes everywhere.

Everything is written from scratch in NumPy. The only library calls are for reading and writing images, plus np.roll for shifting. The blur, the pyramid, both metrics, the gradient features and the border detection are all implemented by hand.

How alignment works

Step 1: cut the plate into channels

The scan is read, converted to floats in [0, 1] (divided by 255 for the 8-bit JPGs and by 65535 for the 16-bit TIFs), and its height is divided by three. The top third is blue, the middle is green, the bottom is red. Blue is kept fixed as the reference, and green and red are aligned to it separately.

Step 2: scoring a shift

A single number is needed that says how similar a shifted channel looks to blue. The shift itself is done with np.roll, which moves the channel dx columns right and dy rows down and wraps whatever falls off the edge back around to the other side. Two scoring options were implemented:

  • L2 / SSD. Subtract the two images pixel by pixel, square everything, add it up: np.sum((a - b)**2). Zero means identical, so lower is better. It's fast, but it has a weakness: if one channel is just uniformly darker than the other, which happens because the filters don't let through equal amounts of light, every single pixel counts as error even when the edges line up perfectly.
  • NCC. Normalised cross-correlation is a dot product between the whole shifted channel and the whole blue channel, treating each as one long vector. Before the dot product, each image's mean is subtracted and the result is divided by its L2 norm, which is the "normalised" part. The score lands between −1 and 1, higher is better, and 1 means the two channels have exactly the same pattern. Because the mean and the norm have been factored out, NCC compares the pattern of variation rather than the absolute values, so it doesn't care if one filter let through more light than another.

Two practical things turned out to matter a lot. First, only the middle of the image is scored, the inner 80 % of each channel (10 % cropped from every side), because the picture doesn't fill the file all the way to the edge. The borders are full of black strips, torn emulsion and scanner white, and those are so high-contrast that when they were left in, the search aligned the borders instead of the picture. Second, since np.roll wraps, the first few rows and columns after a shift are junk from the opposite edge. Cropping the interior takes care of that too, as long as the shift is smaller than the margin.

Step 3: single-scale search (the brute-force version)

With a scoring function in hand, the naive version is short. Every (dx, dy) in a [−15, 15] window in both directions is tried: the moving channel is rolled by that amount, scored against blue, and the best is kept. That's 961 candidates, and on the small JPGs it runs in well under a second per channel.

def align_single_scale(moving, ref, center=(0, 0), radius=15, metric='ncc'):
    ref_c = crop_interior(ref)
    best, best_score = center, None
    for dy in range(cy - radius, cy + radius + 1):
        for dx in range(cx - radius, cx + radius + 1):
            s = score(crop_interior(shift(moving, dx, dy)), ref_c)
            if best_score is None or is_better(s, best_score):
                best, best_score = (dx, dy), s
    return best

The function takes a center and a radius rather than always searching around zero. That small design choice is what lets it be reused inside the pyramid later.

A quick note on the (dx, dy) convention, because it is easy to get backwards. dx is the shift in columns (positive means right), dy is the shift in rows (positive means down). NumPy indexes arrays as (row, column), so the one place the order flips is inside the shift helper, which calls np.roll(im, (dy, dx), axis=(0, 1)). Everywhere an offset is printed, it's (dx, dy).

Step 4: the image pyramid (the fast version)

Brute force stops working on the full-size TIFs. They are about 3200 × 3700 pixels per channel, roughly ten times the size of the JPGs, so the offsets are roughly ten times larger too and a [−15, 15] window is nowhere near enough. Widening it to [−200, 200] would mean 160 000 candidates, each one touching 12 million pixels, which works out to hours per image. Something smarter is needed. (The offsets that were eventually found bear this out: the largest, on melons, was 178 pixels.)

The idea is to solve the problem on a tiny version of the image first, then refine. If the image is shrunk by half, the offset shrinks by half too. Shrunk four times, an offset of 160 pixels becomes 10 pixels, which brute force handles easily. Then the pyramid is walked back up: at each bigger level the estimate from below is doubled and only a tiny window around it (+-2 pixels, chosen by me) is searched to fix the rounding. This is the Gaussian pyramid from lecture (Burt and Adelson, 1983): recursively filter the image, then subsample, until the minimum resolution is reached. Because the image gets re-filtered at every level, the filter itself stays constant and small.

Shrinking properly. Simply deleting every other row and column is undersampling. The fine detail doesn't disappear cleanly, it aliases: it shows up in disguise as jagged, noisy patterns that were never in the original, and two independently aliased images won't match each other. The fix from lecture is to remove the high frequencies with a lowpass filter before sampling. The lowpass filter used here is the binomial kernel [1 4 6 4 1] / 16, which approximates a Gaussian with σ = 1 pixel. It is applied along the rows and then along the columns, written as five shifted NumPy slices rather than a library convolution, and only then is every other pixel kept. At the image border the filter window falls off the edge, so the image is reflect-padded first, which avoids inventing a dark or wrapped-around border that the alignment might latch onto.

The recursion. The whole pyramid is the single-scale function plus five lines:

def align_pyramid(moving, ref, min_size=300, coarse_radius=20, fine_radius=2):
    if min(moving.shape) <= min_size:                       # base case, ~200 px image
        return align_single_scale(moving, ref, (0, 0), coarse_radius)
    dx, dy = align_pyramid(downsample(moving), downsample(ref))   # solve at half size
    return align_single_scale(moving, ref, (2*dx, 2*dy), fine_radius)  # refine here

Reading it from the base case up: if the image is small, it is brute-forced with a wide window. Otherwise both images are downsampled, the function recurses, whatever comes back is doubled, and a +-2 search is done around that at the current size. For a 3200-pixel channel that's five levels: 3209 → 1605 → 803 → 402 → 201. The +-20 search at the 201-pixel level covers +-320 pixels at full resolution, and the four +-2 refinements are 25 candidates each. Only the last 25 ever touch the full image. Each TIF takes 11–14 seconds, a good chunk of which is just loading the 70 MB file, comfortably inside the one-minute target.

My original intention was to use a 100 pixel base level for the coarse brute-force search, but a 100 pixel image does not seem to have enough structure to be reliable, and with a wrong coarse estimate the +-2 pixel refinement cannot fix it. Stopping at about 200 pixels and widening the coarse window to +-20 fixed every image except Emir.

Why Emir fails

The Emir's robe is a saturated blue, and the failure makes sense once each filter's effect on it is considered. The blue filter lets blue light through, so in the blue exposure the robe is almost white. The red filter blocks blue light, so in the red exposure the robe is almost black. The robe takes up a big chunk of the frame, and across that whole region the two channels are anti-correlated: where one is bright, the other is dark.

NCC can undo a constant brightness offset and a contrast difference, but it can't flip the sign of a region. At the true alignment the robe contributes a big negative term, so the true alignment scores badly, and the search wanders off to (−205, 141), where some wrapped-around border content happens to correlate better than the actual picture does. Green, whose robe brightness is somewhere in between, aligns fine.

Bells & whistles

Better features: match edges, not brightness

The Emir failure points at what to fix. Raw brightness is unreliable across channels, but where the brightness changes is not: the outline of the robe, the embroidery, the folds are in the same place in every channel no matter how bright the robe is in that channel. So instead of matching pixel values, edge strength is matched.

The image is treated as a function f(x, y) and partial derivatives are taken with the central-difference kernel [−1 0 1] / 2 from lecture. That approximates ∂f/∂x as (f(x+1, y) − f(x−1, y)) / 2, and the same kernel turned on its side gives ∂f/∂y. In code it's just gx = (I[:, 2:] − I[:, :−2]) / 2 and likewise for gy. The gradient magnitude sqrt(gx² + gy²) is then a map of how fast the image is changing at each pixel, big on edges and near zero on flat regions, no matter which filter the plate was shot through. Derivatives amplify noise, but every coarse level of the pyramid has already been through the Gaussian blur, so only the final full-resolution +-2 refinement ever sees an unsmoothed derivative. The magnitude maps are fed into the same NCC pyramid, completely unchanged, and Emir's red channel snaps to (40, 107).

Using gradients for every image was also tried. On eleven of the fourteen images the gradient offsets match the raw-pixel ones to within a pixel, but on religous_painting and three_generations they land 2–3 pixels off and leave a faint fringe that the raw-pixel result doesn't have. The most likely explanation: gradient magnitude throws away the sign of the edge, and on images full of fine repetitive texture (the cracked canvas of the painting, the wood grain of the pole) lots of edges look alike, so the correlation peak gets flat and the search can slide a couple of pixels. Blurring before taking the gradient didn't help. Keeping the signed gx and gy was also tried, which fixed the painting, but the quick implementation laid them side by side so the shift wrapped gx into gy and Emir broke again, and it doubled the runtime, so it was dropped. The final configuration is raw pixels by default, with gradient magnitude as the fallback for images like Emir. Both sets of offsets are reported.

Automatic cropping

The aligned images all have an ugly rainbow frame around the edge. Cutting a fixed margin off would be cheating, so instead the border is detected on the aligned RGB image using two cues. First, the rows and columns that np.roll wrapped around (the first |dx| columns and |dy| rows of the largest shift) are known junk, so those are removed immediately. Second, every remaining row and column is checked for whether more than half of it is near-black or near-white on some channel, or whether the three channels disagree by more than 0.3 on average. Real content agrees across channels; the plate edge is black in one and white in another. The scan walks inward from each side while a line looks like border, stops at the first good line, and never removes more than 12 % of the image so a genuinely dark scene survives.

Automatic contrast and white balance

Two finishing touches are applied. First, contrast stretching. The raw plates have a low-contrast histogram bunched up in the middle of the range, so intensities are remapped linearly so that the 0.5th percentile goes to 0 and the 99.5th to 1, which spreads the histogram out to fill the whole [0, 1] range. The percentiles are computed over all three channels together so hue doesn't shift. Second, gray-world white balance, following the approach described in the Wikipedia article on colour balance and in section 2.3.2 of Szeliski, Computer Vision: Algorithms and Applications: assume the average colour of the scene should be grey, and scale each channel so its mean equals the mean of all three. It's a crude illuminant estimate, but it removes most of the yellow-green cast the raw plates have.

Code

All the code is in one file, colorize.py, with a small command-line interface:

python colorize.py "data/*.jpg" --method single --metric ncc          # single-scale on the JPGs
python colorize.py "data/*.tif" --method pyramid --metric ncc         # pyramid, raw pixels
python colorize.py "data/emir.tif" --feature gradient --post          # gradient features + crop/contrast/WB

Each run prints the (dx, dy) displacement for green and red plus the time taken, saves the colour image as a JPG, and dumps all the offsets to a JSON file.
